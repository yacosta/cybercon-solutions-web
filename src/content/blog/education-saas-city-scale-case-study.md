A citywide education program only works if families can trust the platform — and if the platform can grow without falling over. Before founding Cybercon Solutions, Yezid Acosta led technology for a New York City education nonprofit running a wealth-building program for public-school families: live for **145,000 families**, architected to scale past **1.1 million** students.

Here’s what he found, what he shipped, and what he’d tell another education tech leader in the same seat.

## What he walked into

The product already mattered. Families depended on it. External partners watched outcomes. City technology and cybersecurity stakeholders expected clean SaaS integration and documented controls.

What needed sharpening was ownership: who defined success metrics, who owned security against student and family data rules, and who sat in the middle when program teams, vendors, and city offices disagreed about “done.”

If you run [education or nonprofit IT](/industries/education-nonprofits/), that mix will sound familiar — mission urgency, public scrutiny, and compliance that doesn’t wait for a quiet quarter.

## Platform leadership at city scale

As the technology leader, he directed the technology infrastructure behind the program platform. The job wasn’t a greenfield rebuild. It was keeping a live system healthy while the enrollment curve pointed at citywide scale.

That meant capacity planning, vendor coordination, integration discipline, and a clear path for what had to work on day one versus what could wait. Growth without architecture is just a longer outage list.

## Product ownership that finance and partners could read

He also served as product owner for the outcome-tracking product used to measure post-delivery results: defining the metrics, tracking ROI after delivery, and reporting performance to executive leadership and external partners — sitting between program and technology so neither side had to guess what “good” meant.

That reporting habit is what keeps a platform fundable. Partners don’t buy effort. They buy evidence.

## Security and compliance for student and family data

Confidential student and family data sat in the middle of everything. He implemented cybersecurity frameworks under strict **FERPA** expectations and the city’s technology and cybercommand standards — not a binder on a shelf, but controls vendors and consultants had to live inside.

He managed a diverse vendor and consultant ecosystem so security didn’t get negotiated away in the last mile of a contract.

## Bridging program teams and city technology offices

He was the primary point of contact for the city’s technology and innovation office, cybercommand stakeholders, and the education department on SaaS integration and regulatory compliance. Someone had to translate program language into security language — and back — without losing either.

That liaison work is unglamorous. It’s also why launches don’t stall for six months in email threads.

## What we’d tell another education executive

Name the crown jewels early — family identity, student data, enrollment paths, outcome reporting. Put a human owner on the product metrics partners will ask about. Treat city and regulator stakeholders as part of delivery, not an afterthought.

And don’t let “scale someday” wait until enrollment forces an emergency. Design for the number you’re promising now.
